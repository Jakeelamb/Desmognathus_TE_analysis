# Independent AI review: evolutionary cell biology and genome-size evolution

**Assessment.** The strongest biological result is coordinated genome-size signal and erythrocyte size within *Desmognathus*, accompanied by imperfect nuclear–cell proportionality. The interesting evolutionary contribution is the separation of this cellular association from the more taxon-sensitive relationship with adult body size. It supports a nucleotypic interpretation as one plausible explanation, while leaving the origin of genome-size differences and the direction of cellular causation unresolved.

I inspected the rendered Figures 5, 7, 9, and 10, their numerical sources, Figure 8's joined traits and regression coefficients, and the morphology methods. This review uses existing results only; no analyses or figures were rerun or modified.

## What the data establish

Figure 5 shows the clearest association between the conditional genome-size estimate and cell area: standardized log-trait PGLS β = 0.717, 95% interval 0.408–1.025, P = 0.0000816, n = 24. Its generalized R² is 0.514, versus 0.176 for genome–nucleus area. The latter association is positive but weaker: β = 0.419, interval 0.018–0.820. Cell and nuclear areas covary, β = 0.579, interval 0.219–0.940. These estimates describe retained large erythrocytes; they are not measurements of every cell type or the average circulating erythrocyte.

Body adjustment does not dissolve the genome–cell association. In Figure 8's `c ~ g + b`, genome β = 0.545 [0.192, 0.899], P = 0.00421; excluding *aeneus* and *wrighti* gives 0.503 [0.041, 0.965]. Figure 9's equal-animal alternatives give 0.583 [0.225, 0.941] and 0.468 [0.004, 0.933], respectively. Thus, the positive association extends beyond the two smallest direct developers, although the 22-species estimate is substantially less precise. The most useful finding is this persistence across biologically meaningful changes in taxon membership and animal weighting.

For nuclear area, adding genome size to cell area and SVL yields genome β = 0.051 [−0.499, 0.600], while cell β = 0.652 [0.074, 1.230]. This shows little resolved *additional predictive information* in genome size after those covariates. It does not establish the absence of a direct DNA effect: the genome interval includes appreciable positive and negative coefficients. Nor does it establish that cells determine nuclei. Shared developmental regulation, feedback, correlated errors, and alternative causal directions remain compatible with these conditional associations.

Figure 9 strengthens the covariance result: the cell-bridge candidate ranks first in 216/250 measurement resamples and all 24 single-species-removal fits. Those outcomes quantify stability within four candidates that all place SVL upstream. They are not probabilities that the indicated biological arrows are true. The visible arrows remain particularly easy to overread as mechanisms.

## Nuclear–cell scaling and allocation

Figure 10's unstandardized, response-on-predictor log–log slope is 0.450 [0.170, 0.730], with P(slope = 1) = 0.000504. Released/equal-animal and 24/22-species point estimates range from 0.397 to 0.483; all four conditional intervals exclude one. The 1,000 paired resampling fits have median 0.465 and central 95% range 0.306–0.633. This is consistent evidence that, in this selected panel and regression orientation, nuclear area increases less steeply than cell area.

The separate paired-fraction display makes the biology more concrete. Equal-animal species means of animal median nucleus/cell area fractions span 13.64% in *brimleyorum* to 19.12% in *aeneus*. *Wrighti* is 17.93%, overlapping the aquatic-larval *orestes* at 18.12%. There is no visually clean developmental-mode partition. Animal heterogeneity is also visible: *mavrokoilius* animal medians are approximately 14.23% and 18.05%. These are two-dimensional allocations, not nuclear/cytoplasmic volume ratios or measurements of biosynthetic investment.

The slope is not a demonstrated structural exponent. Predictor error, axis choice, selection for large cell area, and biological scatter affect a conditional slope. The resampling cannot remove those issues. Likewise, a ratio incorporates its component measurements; it should not become an independent causal trait or be regressed against its denominator as proof of allocation regulation. Figure 9 summarizes morphology by the **median** of animal medians; Figure 10 uses their **arithmetic mean**. Both are defensible sensitivities, but their estimands should remain explicit.

## Evolutionary interpretation and literature

The genus-specific precedent is important. Hally and colleagues examined four desmognathine species using Feulgen cytophotometry with an internal *Xenopus* reference. They found significant but small DNA-content differences that did not support the proposed life-history relationships. The present 24-species panel can extend that limited taxon coverage; genome-size variation in this genus itself is not a new discovery. Historical taxonomic circumscriptions and calibration also preclude a casual one-to-one numerical comparison. [Hally et al. 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3744902/)

Jockusch's 15-species plethodontid study found a positive genome-size/embryonic-development-time association, but explicitly could not distinguish selection on nucleotypic properties from accumulation of DNA constrained by fitness effects. That distinction fits this manuscript: a nucleotypic **effect** of DNA amount need not mean that DNA amount was adaptively increased. Direct development is a developmental mode, not a measurement of developmental speed; these data contain no embryonic duration, division-rate, or metabolic measurements. [Jockusch 1997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1688400/)

Roth, Blanke, and Wake found that small salamanders with large cells had especially simple tectal morphology. Their result provides a concrete hypothesis for why cellular dimensions could constrain the consequences of miniaturization. It does not allow erythrocyte area here to stand in for neural complexity or tissue cell number. Smaller cells might alleviate packing constraints in small bodies, but this study measures neither the relevant organs nor that functional benefit. [Roth et al. 1994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC43875/)

The conditional prominence of cell area is biologically plausible without excluding DNA effects. Levy and Heald experimentally reproduced interspecies nuclear scaling in *Xenopus* egg extracts and manipulated nuclear import factors to alter nuclear size. Cytoplasmic regulation can therefore contribute to nuclear dimensions. This is a mechanistic precedent for multiple determinants, not evidence that the same factors explain mature *Desmognathus* erythrocytes or that the selected path direction is correct. [Levy and Heald 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2966892/)

Figure 7's body–genome β changes from 0.667 (P = 0.0081) to 0.418 (P = 0.152), and body–cell β from 0.611 (P = 0.0015) to 0.338 (P = 0.142), when both direct developers are omitted. These remain positive point estimates. The comparison narrows body-size range and simultaneously changes development and taxon membership. It neither proves a developmental-mode effect nor proves that body-size association disappears. *Aeneus* (31 mm maximum SVL; conditional genome estimate 9.62 pg) and *wrighti* (29 mm; 14.79 pg) also differ markedly despite similar maximum sizes: miniaturization need not correspond to a single genome-size solution.

## Main objection and manuscript claim

The main objection is the biological interpretation of the measurements. The genome trait is assembly-anchored IOD, with unresolved standard-to-run mapping and an approximately 41% difference between the two standard-slide median IODs. A common multiplicative anchor cannot change standardized log associations; species-specific staining or batch effects can. Morphology targets the 50 largest eligible cells, seven species have only one animal, and *wrighti* contributes eight paired cells. Animal resampling improves transparency but cannot recover population sampling, unobserved animals, cross-assay covariance, or calibration uncertainty. Consequently, calibration uncertainty is more consequential for the nucleotypic claim than the nominal P values.

**Best manuscript claim:** “Across the selected erythrocyte panel, conditional genome-size estimates covary positively with cell area after accounting for species maximum body size. Nuclear area covaries with cell area, while paired nuclear fractions vary among species; these patterns persist under specified animal-weighting and taxon-removal sensitivities.”

Three evolutionary explanations remain viable: DNA accumulation with cellular consequences and organismal constraints; selection on cellular/developmental properties that indirectly limits genome size; and coordinated regulation of genome, cell, and nuclear traits by other lineage characteristics. Existing results distinguish robust cellular covariance from more leverage-sensitive body associations. They do not distinguish adaptation from constraint, identify a causal sequence, or demonstrate developmental and physiological consequences.
